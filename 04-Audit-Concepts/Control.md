A **control** is a policy, procedure, practice, or mechanism designed to provide reasonable assurance that objectives will be achieved and risks mitigated. Controls may be preventive (stop errors or fraud before they occur), detective (identify issues after they occur), or corrective (remediate and improve). They operate at entity, process, and transaction levels.

Auditors evaluate [[Design Effectiveness]] (whether the control, if operated as intended, would address the [[Control Objective]] / [[Risk]]) and [[Operating Effectiveness]] (whether it actually functioned over the [[Audit Period]] at the required [[Control Frequency]], by the right person/system, with sufficient evidence). See [[Control Testing]].

Control testing may use [[Walkthrough]], [[Inquiry]], [[Observation (Procedure)]], [[Inspection]], [[Reperformance]], or [[Analytics]]. **[[Inquiry]] alone does not demonstrate operating effectiveness** for the period—corroborate with other [[Evidence]]. A walkthrough is primarily a design/understanding tool, not full-period OE testing. Documentation that a control exists is not proof it operated ([[Control Ownership]]).

Weak or missing controls are a [[Control Deficiency]]; they increase [[Risk]] and may support [[Finding]]s when paired with evidence of adverse conditions—but not every deficiency is reportable.

Control frameworks (e.g., COSO internal control components, COBIT for IT) help structure reviews. [[IT Controls]] and [[Security Controls]] are specialized subsets. Clear [[Control Ownership]] supports accountability for design, operation, and remediation.

Controls are not guarantees; they reduce risk to an acceptable level within cost and operational constraints. Over-reliance on manual controls, compensating controls, or undocumented practices are common review themes in professional audit literature.
